Home Passport

(the story)

Home Passport is an app concept for Frontdoor and American Home Shield. It keeps every appliance, warranty, and repair in one place, and registers a new device with a single NFC tap.

(my role)

I worked on this with my teammate Anahi. I researched the existing appliance apps, mapped the service flows, designed the screens, built the design system, and made the physical NFC tag prototype. We presented to Frontdoor at the final review, where it was selected as a recognized proposal.

(the problem)

There was no reason to register in the first place.

Half of U.S. homes own a smart appliance, but only about 1 in 10 ever connect it to its app. I tested LG ThinQ, Samsung SmartThings, and the current AHS app to understand why. All three ask for long forms, serial numbers, and purchase details before giving anything back. Booking one repair takes 12 steps, and the app remembers none of it next time.

The interface was never the real problem.

Behind the scenes...

NFC onboarding

Tap, confirm, done

Registration had to cost nothing, so I designed it around a physical NFC tag on the appliance. Your phone touches the tag, the appliance identifies itself, and the home record updates before you open a form.

I storyboarded the cases that usually break this kind of flow, new appliances, second-hand ones, older machines with an unreadable label, so the same three steps work for all of them. The record belongs to the home rather than the owner, so it survives when the house is sold.

The dashboard

How is my home doing right now?

Once registration is free, the app has something to give back. I built the dashboard around one question: how is my home doing right now? Health first, then age, last service, and efficiency for each device.

The alerts speak in money rather than error codes, your fridge coils are dusty, save $15 a month. Manuals and receipts go into a paperless drawer you can export when you sell.

Every card on this screen resizes from one component. The appliance card is built with auto layout and constraints so it holds at any width, and the status chip is a variant rather than a separate layer, so a device moving from healthy to overdue is a property change, not a redraw.

The audit

What the current app actually does

Before rebuilding the design system I wanted to know what the current app actually does. I captured twenty screens of the live AHS app and logged every button, colour, and label in a spreadsheet, one row per element with its colour, size, and source screen.

Then I checked each colour pair against WCAG, text against its background, borders against their surface, labels against the buttons they sit on.

Seven primary buttons came back below the standard. The orange with a white label measures 3.77:1 and body text needs 4.5:1. Form borders measure 1.48:1 against a 3:1 requirement. The same primary button appears at four different widths across four screens, and six pairs of colours are close enough that nobody could tell them apart.

Values were typed screen by screen instead of coming from one source, so nothing kept them consistent and nothing caught the failures.

Colour

Checked against the surface it sits on

AHS is orange, and orange reads as urgency, the feeling of something breaking. A home-care app needs the opposite, so I moved the palette to blue. I made that decision before the audit, for what the colour communicates. The audit then showed the orange was failing contrast anyway.

Every new colour was checked against the surface it actually sits on rather than against white.

Design tokens

One value, named three times

A token is a named value. Instead of typing a hex code on a screen, a button asks for action.primary.bg and gets its colour from one place.

I built it in three layers. The base layer holds the raw colours. The middle layer names them by job, action, surface, border. Components only ever ask the middle layer, never the raw colour. That middle layer is what makes theming possible: change it and every screen follows without touching a component.

In Figma the same structure runs on variables and modes, so light and dark are the same components reading a different mode rather than a duplicated page.

Components

Properties, not copies

Each component is one Figma component with properties rather than a page of copies. The button carries variant, size, and state as properties, with a boolean for the icon, so every combination comes from one master. Auto layout handles the padding and constraints handle the resize, which is why the same button works full width on a form and inline in a card.

I built them in code the same way. Variants are props, and the states are real CSS states rather than separate stories, hover is :hover, focus is :focus-visible, so you can tab to the ring instead of looking at a picture of one.

The build

The build stops if a colour fails

Figma pushes to GitHub. One file becomes CSS, Swift, and Android XML. Before anything ships, the system checks every colour pair against the accessibility standard and stops if one fails.

I put the old orange back to confirm it stops. It did, naming the exact colour and the exact ratio.

It also caught two colours I had approved myself. They cleared the standard against white but failed against the page background, I had checked one surface and assumed the other.

Documentation

Usage rules beside the live component

The components run in Storybook, one page each, with every variant and state. The controls panel lets anyone switch variant, size, or state without opening the code, and the accessibility add-on runs a check on every story.

Storybook is embedded in Zeroheight, where the usage rules sit next to the live component, when to use it, when not to, and the accessibility notes. A colour change updates both.

(the outcome)

Selected as a recognized proposal at the final review. Twelve steps became three taps.

What I took away: this was an incentive problem, not a UI problem. A good interface at the wrong moment still fails. Home services will not be won by who fixes things fastest, but by who knows your home best before anything breaks.
