First, what project controls is

Project controls is the job of knowing whether a project is on budget and on schedule while there is still time to act.

It runs on three numbers: the work you planned to have done by now, the work actually done, and what you have spent. Compare work done with money spent and you get the cost index. Compare it with the plan and you get the schedule index.

Below 1.0 means trouble. This program scores 0.94 on cost and 0.91 on schedule.

  • Which projects are over budget?
  • Which are behind schedule?
  • What will it all cost at the end?
  • Where is the biggest risk, and is safety improving?
  • How many people have no project?

Why I built it

Someone who works in project controls told me which skills their teams rely on: cleaning data in Power Query, building a data model, and writing DAX measures. I learn by making things, so I made the real thing.

A program leader asks the same five questions every month. I designed one page that answers all five.

I work on a Mac and Power BI's desktop app is Windows only, so I built all of it in the browser version.

Cleaning the data

The six tables were messy on purpose, the way real project data is. Before any chart, I fixed the data in Power Query.

Power Query records each fix as a step. When new data arrives, every step runs again on its own, so the cleanup is done once and not every month.

Two data-type errors failed without any warning. Dates read in the wrong order, day for month, became different dates. And a whole-number setting rounded 60% up to 1.

The model view in Power BI: six data tables joined through Projects and Date.

The data model

I built a star schema: the Projects table and a Date table sit in the middle, joined to the other tables by 10 one-to-many relationships.

That is what makes the page work as one thing. Pick a project, a month or a region, and every number and chart changes together.

On top of the model I wrote the measures in DAX: CPI (cost index), SPI (schedule index), EAC (forecast final cost), VAC (forecast overrun), risk exposure (chance of each open risk times its cost) and TRIR (recordable safety incidents per 200,000 hours worked).

What the dashboard showed

The program is forecast to finish $18.7M over budget, and one project causes two thirds of that.

That changes what a leader does next. The program does not need fixing everywhere. Columbus West needs attention first.

Safety is not improving. Recordable incidents trended upward through 2026, and the rate stands at 3.96.

The largest open risk is losing the windows when power lines can be switched off for work, at $5.6M. About 17 of 61 staff had no project in a typical month.

Three numbers I had to get right

Three months had work recorded but no cost. I counted them, and it made projects look cheaper than they were. Once I left those months out, Fayette went from under budget to over.

Then I added up monthly headcount and got 354 people without a project, out of 61 staff. Headcount is a snapshot, so the right figure is the monthly average: 17.

The cost index is worked out for the whole program from totals, not by averaging the projects. Averaging would count a $3.6M project the same as a $78M one.

The wrong numbers all looked fine on the page. So I checked every figure against a separate calculation in Python.

What is still unfinished

It is not finished. The staffing chart still labels unassigned staff as "(Blank)", and I have not published the report yet.

All data is synthetic and does not describe any real utility or company.

What I learned

  • A dashboard is a design problem as much as a data problem.
  • The hard part is the data underneath, not the charts.
  • A number can look right on the page and still be wrong.

How I'll use it next

  • Check every figure I put on a screen against its source, in design work too.
  • Use the same data modelling in Reach and in future analysis projects.
